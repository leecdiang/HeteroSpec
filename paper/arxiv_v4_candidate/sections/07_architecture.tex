\section{Architectural implications}
\label{sec:architecture}
The experiments support a constrained architectural lesson rather than an accelerator claim. First, reference-preserving local control can retain batching outside attention, but a backend's actual operator implementation determines whether the transformed view is both supported and numerically admissible. Second, a faster verification phase does not establish lower request cost: draft prefill, state maintenance, context creation and teardown can dominate. Third, a cheap dispatch path is not an optimization if it fails the registered reference.

The RTX closure panel is instructive because its two conclusions differ. QB's incremental layout effect is unresolved under 5.21\% A/A imbalance, while QB speculative service is 41.8\% lower than target-only service for the four fixed inputs. The latter supports the tested speculative policy on that panel; it does not isolate QB as the cause of the gain. The B570 panel, under its own execution policy and cost contract, passes semantic qualification but does not establish a QB/PQ layout benefit and does not repay draft cost. These results must remain separate rather than being averaged into a platform-independent number.

A hardware study would require evidence not yet present: a repeatable residual after the strongest qualified software baseline, a dependency timeline showing that the residual lies on the request critical path, and physical counters or interventions that discriminate among memory traffic, reduction work, launch/synchronization and host-side costs. Only then can a calibrated cycle/traffic model and equal-resource RTL/PPA comparison be justified. The present evidence does not identify such a residual, nor does it establish area, frequency, energy or bandwidth advantages. We therefore retain a software-first position.
